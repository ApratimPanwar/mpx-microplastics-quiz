MPx Spin

A countertop centrifuge for drinking water. You pour a 400 mL batch into a spinning steel basket lined with a paper sleeve; the spin pushes the water through the sleeve, the sleeve keeps the microplastics, and afterwards the sleeve itself is the sample you look at under the Carson MP-250 pocket microscope on an iPhone.

How it works

Water in solid-body rotation forms a paraboloid in the basket; the pressure at the wall drives it through the wet sleeve (Darcy flow). The base is closed, so everything that leaves passes the paper.

  1. Pour a batch400 mL through the Ø16 lid chute into the 316L basket. A reed switch proves the lid is shut and (v1.1) a solenoid holds it until the basket stops.
  2. SpinA 24 V BLDC turns the basket. v1 holds 1,500 rpm; v1.1 follows a speed profile up to 2,500 rpm that keeps the face velocity on the paper at 5× its rating.
  3. Filter through the sleeveA cellulose sleeve (Grade 602 h, < 2 µm; two layers in v1.1) on a 50 µm 316L mesh wrap. Four welded vanes make the water turn with the basket.
  4. CollectClean water flies off the cage into the 304 bowl and runs out of the spout into the jug. Two pins in the spout sense when the batch has drained.
  5. Lift out the sampleThe sleeve lifts out whole. Stain with Nile red and read it with the Carson MP-250 and an iPhone, with a size bar in frame.

The rig experiment that calibrated the model

A Ø50 × 150 mm rig with 25 µm paper on a 50 µm mesh, 200 mL per run, three runs per condition. The motor-off run fixes the paper's lumped permeance; the ≈ 5,000 rpm run shows the water only turned at 6.5 % of the motor speed. With those two numbers the model predicts the 3,500 rpm run.

What it changed. Because the water lagged the basket so badly, Spin v1 adds four welded vanes, assumed to bring the water to 85 % of the basket speed. The rig also passed water 3.2× faster than the paper datasheet allows, which points to water bypassing the paper at the seam or the ends (FMEA 2). Measuring the real water speed with vanes is bench test 1.

The model behind it

Water in the spinning basket sits in solid-body rotation, so its free surface is a paraboloid z = z₀ + ω²r²/2g clipped by the basket, and the pressure on the wall at height z is ρg(z_wall − z). Flow through the wet wall is Darcy's law with the paper's permeance (Whatman Herzberg times), the base closed. The rig fixes two unknowns: its lumped permeance from the motor-off run (22.4 s) and, from the ≈ 5,000 rpm run (6.8 s), how fast the water actually turns (6.5 % of the motor speed). With those, the model predicts the 3,500 rpm run at 10.9 s against 10.35 s measured. Vanes are assumed to make the water turn at 85 % of the basket; that, the cake resistance and the paper's capture at high face velocity are what the next bench tests must measure.

Speed and paper choice

Faster spinning raises flow and sleeve life but pushes water through the paper faster than its rating. 1,000–1,750 rpm is the band where flow passes and the face velocity stays reasonable.

Physical contradictions

PC1

Pores must be small (catch 1 µm) and large (pass 15 L/h)

Separated in Space

two layers in series: each stays as open as 602 h, together they catch 98.9 % at 1–2 µm

PC2

The basket must spin fast (flow) and slowly (paper rating, noise)

Separated in Time

speed profile: about 1,250 rpm when full, rising to 2,500 rpm as the water thins

PC3

Particles must reach the paper and float inward (PP, PE > 20 µm)

Separated in Condition

slow, steady stop lets the floating layer settle onto the sleeve; count a floor coupon too

PC4

The sleeve must be sealed and removable

Separated in Time + self-service (25)

spin pressure presses the 20 mm overlaps shut; floor gasket and clamp ring seal the ends; lifts out when stopped
