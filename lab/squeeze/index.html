MPx Squeeze

A pocket microplastic filter that screws onto any standard PET bottle, or onto a tap. Squeeze the bottle and the water passes a 20 µm steel prefilter and a ≈ 1 µm sintered 316L disc. Afterwards the disc lifts out flat and is itself the microscope slide. No plastic touches the water.

How it works

Four ways to use one part set (v2). Hand force becomes bottle pressure; Darcy flow through the prefilter, the cake and the disc in series sets the flow.

  1. SqueezeBottle inverted, ~0.2 bar at 60 N. Water passes the 20 µm prefilter cup in the neck, then the disc cassette, out of the push-pull spout.
  2. On a tapTap adapters (M24 / M22 / universal ferrule) put 1–2 bar on the same cassette: over 100 L/h.
  3. BackflushReverse flow for 10–20 s through the coupler on the spout stem washes ~97 % of the cake off; catch it in a dish.
  4. InspectUnscrew the cap, lift the cassette into the holder, cover glass, phone lens. Nile red + blue LED for particles under 20 µm.
Two size fractions for analysis: the prefilter keeps ≥ 20 µm (backflush it into a dish); the disc keeps ~1–20 µm, about 96 % of microplastics by count.

The model behind it

Hand force becomes bottle pressure as (force − 4 N wall stiffness) ÷ 34 cm² palm contact, plus the water column. Flow through the element is Darcy's law with the sintered media's resistance from Mott's KL, a cake that grows with the solids in the water (specific resistance 1e13 m/kg), and a little irreversible fouling left by each backflush. Capture by size follows a sharp curve around the media's liquid rating, and microplastics are assumed at 1,000 per litre (1–100 µm, count ∝ size⁻²). Palm contact, cake resistance and particle count are assumptions that bench tests 2, 3 and 7 will replace.

Findings in five stepsMPx Squeeze v1 · test bench
1What

MPx Squeeze v1 is a stainless steel body screwed between a standard PET bottle (28 PCO 1881 thread) and a drinking spout. Inside is a closed-end sintered 316L tube (Ø20 × 83.5 mm, grade 0.2, about 1.4 µm in water). You squeeze the bottle; water passes from outside to inside through the steel wall, and the particles stay on the outside.

Principle tested: a hand squeezing a bottle makes enough pressure (about 0.2 bar) to push water through sintered steel fine enough to catch 1–2 µm microplastics, and tap water run backwards cleans it.

2Why

Sintered steel at ~1 µm is tough, washable and has no plastic in the water path, but it resists flow strongly. Before ordering custom tubes we needed to know how hard you must squeeze, how quickly the particle cake clogs it, and whether backflushing on a tap restores the flow.

3How

Simulation. Hand force becomes bottle pressure: (force − 4 N bottle stiffness) ÷ 34 cm² palm contact, plus the water column. Flow through the element follows Darcy's law using the supplier's (Mott) permeability coefficient KL (grade 0.2: KL 26; grade 0.5: KL 8), a 2 mm wall and 55.6 cm² of filter area. A cake grows with the solids in the water (resistance 1 × 10¹³ m/kg), and each backflush leaves a little permanent fouling. Capture follows a sharp curve around the media's rating. 1,000 microplastics per litre are assumed (1–100 µm, count ∝ size⁻²).

Variables: hand force 0–120 N, tap pressure, bottle 500 mL or 1 L, element grade 0.2 or 0.5, water type (tap, borewell, turbid), phone lens 50× or 140×. Constraints: v1 drawing-set dimensions, a comfortable squeeze of 60 N, a target of 0.4 L/min (24 L/h).

4Results
  • At a comfortable 60 N, grade 0.2 gives 0.33 L/min (≈ 20 L/h); reaching 0.4 L/min needs 73 N. 500 mL takes about 90 s.
  • Catches 98 % of 1–2 µm particles and ~100 % above 2 µm. Grade 0.5 flows three times faster but catches only 35 % at 1–2 µm.
  • Flow halves after about 26 L of tap water. On a tap at 1.5 bar: 2.7 L/min. Body wall safety factor 39× at 3 bar.
5So what
  • The filter physics work, but v1 is long (155 mm outside the bottle), needs a firm squeeze, and its cake sits on a curved tube that has to be rinsed off before the phone lens can see it.
  • Squeeze v2 answers each point: a flat disc that is itself the microscope slide, a built-in 20 µm prefilter to protect it, a 50 mm body and an air valve.
Software and method. Our own physics model, written in JavaScript (model.mjs) and run live in your browser; the drawing, charts and requirement checks are all computed from it. No MATLAB, COMSOL, ANSYS or CFD was used: the model is first-principles equations solved step by step in code. Where a number could not be calculated, it is an assumption the bench labels, and a physical bench test is planned to replace it.

Squeeze v2 · what changed from v1

  • Envelope Ø52 × 50 mm outside the bottle (v1: Ø35 × 155.5 mm).
  • Two stages: a built-in 20 µm prefilter cup that sits inside the bottle neck, then one removable disc cassette (~1 µm) that comes out flat for the microscope.
  • Tube element, body, element base and dust cap removed: 5 core parts (v1: 9).
  • Cassettes A / B / C share one frame, so the disc grade is a user choice.
  • An air valve lets the bottle refill between squeezes: without it the wet disc (2.9 bar bubble point) holds a vacuum and the bottle stalls after about one squeeze.

The model behind it

Every number comes from the model that moves the drawing. Hand force becomes bottle pressure as (force − wall stiffness) ÷ palm contact, plus the water column; water viscosity follows temperature. Flow is Darcy's law through the prefilter, the cake and the disc in series (disc media from Mott KL; the asymmetric disc A taken at 13 KL·mm, 3× a standard 1.5 mm grade-0.2 disc). Each squeeze can push out at most 30 % of the bottle before the hand closes; without a valve the bottle keeps its dent and pulls 0.6 bar of vacuum per unit of volume lost, because the wet disc needs about 2.9 bar before air can pass it. Capture by size follows a sharp curve around each rating. Grey sintered steel hides particles under about 20 µm in white light; Nile red makes plastic glow so the lens limit applies.

Findings in five stepsMPx Squeeze v2 · test bench
1What

MPx Squeeze v2 screws onto any PET bottle (or a tap). Water passes a built-in 20 µm steel prefilter cup, then a removable Ø40 sintered 316L disc (cassette A, asymmetric, about 1 µm), and leaves through a push-pull spout. After use the cassette lifts out flat and the disc is the microscope slide.

Principle tested: two-stage, all-steel filtration driven only by a hand squeeze, with the catch kept on a flat surface that a phone lens can read.

2Why

Squeeze v1 showed the physics work, but its tube was long, clogged after about 26 L and was awkward to inspect. Before ordering custom asymmetric discs we needed to know:

  • the force needed for 15 L/h with each cassette,
  • how fast the disc clogs and how much backflushing gives back,
  • whether the bottle refills with air between squeezes (a wet fine disc will not let air through),
  • whether particles can be seen on grey steel with a phone lens.
3How

Simulation. Hand force becomes bottle pressure (palm contact 34 cm², bottle stiffness, water column); water viscosity follows temperature. Flow follows Darcy's law through the prefilter, the cake and the disc in series (disc permeability from Mott's KL data; the asymmetric disc A taken at 13 KL·mm). One squeeze can push out at most 30 % of the bottle. Without a valve the dented bottle pulls a vacuum (0.6 bar per unit volume lost), and air cannot get back through the wet disc until about 2.9 bar (its bubble point). Each stage catches particles along its own capture curve; the prefilter is assumed to take 40 % of the solids; 1,000 microplastics per litre.

Variables: hand force, tap pressure, water temperature, cassette A/B/C, bottle size, water type, air valve on/off, lens, Nile red stain, and four assumptions you can edit (palm contact, cake resistance, prefilter share, particles per litre). Constraints: Ø52 × 50 mm outside the bottle, 38.5 mm active disc, target ≤ 60 N for 15 L/h, 316L and silicone only in the water path.

4Results
  • Cassette A: 15.6 L/h at 58 N. Cassette B (standard grade 0.2) needs 181 N; cassette C is easy to squeeze but lets 65 % of 1–2 µm particles through.
  • Catches 99.5 % of microplastics by count, 99.0 % at 1–2 µm.
  • Flow halves after 2.2 L of tap water; a 10–20 s tap backflush brings back about 97 %.
  • Without the air valve the bottle stalls after about one squeeze. On a tap at 1.5 bar: 132 L/h. At 5 °C flow drops 41 %.
5So what
  • Keep cassette A and the air valve.
  • Filter life is the weak point: test a prefilter that takes more of the solids (90 % → 13 L before flow halves), a Ø44 disc, and backflushing as a routine; add an end-of-life cue.
  • The force (58 N) is just above the 50 N wish. Bench tests 1–7 measure palm contact, cake resistance and prefilter share to replace the assumptions.
